How to quit vaping, step by step

Most vapes contain nicotine, and nicotine is addictive, so stopping can be harder than you expect. Here is what makes vaping different, what withdrawal feels like and how long it lasts, and a plan for your quit day and every craving after it.

By Moon Arc Works · Updated November 24, 2026

Why vaping is so hard to put down

Vapes are often treated as harmless, but most contain nicotine, which is highly addictive. The WHO says e-cigarettes are harmful and not safe, and some sold as nicotine-free have been found to contain nicotine.

Many vapes use nicotine salts. The CDC explains that they let people take in high levels of nicotine without the harshness of freebase nicotine, so a strong dose can feel smooth.

And there is no natural stopping point. A cigarette burns down and ends; a vape sits in your pocket all day, so puffs spread across waking up, coffee, messages, boredom and breaks, often without you noticing. That is why the plan below starts with noticing.

Vape withdrawal: what to expect and for how long

When you stop, your brain and body have to get used to going without nicotine. Common symptoms are strong urges to vape, irritability or restlessness, feeling low or anxious, headaches, trouble sleeping or concentrating, and feeling hungrier than usual.

They are temporary: according to smokefree.gov, the worst usually lasts a few days to a couple of weeks. This is the timeline AirAgain shows in vape mode.

20 minutes

Your heart rate and blood pressure start to come down.

1 day

Nicotine in your blood has dropped to almost nothing.

3 days

Withdrawal is usually at its strongest around now. From here it gets easier.

2 weeks

Throat irritation and the cough some vapers get often start to ease.

4 weeks

Most withdrawal symptoms, like irritability, restlessness and poor sleep, have passed.

3 months

The nicotine receptors in your brain are back to normal levels, so cravings become rare.

Sources: NHS, CDC and Cosgrove et al. (2009). Research on vaping is younger than on smoking, so this timeline sticks to what is well established. General information, not medical advice.

Your quit plan

Pick a quit day no more than a week or two away, and not in your most stressful week. In AirAgain, choose vape mode, then quit today or pick a date. Until then, the home screen counts down and gives you short steps to get ready.

All at once or step by step? The NHS describes both. You can cut down first by lowering the nicotine strength of your e-liquid, leaving longer gaps between puffs or making some places vape-free. Or you can stop in one step and ask a pharmacist or stop-smoking adviser about nicotine replacement. AirAgain’s cut-down plan counts cigarettes, so in vape mode you work towards a quit day.

If you vape to stay off cigarettes, the NHS advises quitting vaping only when you are confident you can do it without smoking again. If you both smoke and vape, smokefree.gov recommends quitting both.

Two of the app’s preparation steps, in their version for vapers:

Map your triggers

Vaping is easy to do without noticing: the device is always in your pocket and there is no "end" to a session. But most puffs still follow cues: waking up, coffee, a stressful message, boredom, a break at work.

Once you know your cues, they stop catching you off guard. You can plan for each one before your quit day.

Try this: For one day, note each time you reach for your vape and what happened just before.

Clear everything out

On the evening before your quit day, get rid of everything: devices, pods, refills, disposables and the spare one in your bag or car. Delete shop apps and unsubscribe from vape offers.

Not having a vape within reach puts a few minutes between you and the urge, and a craving usually passes in those minutes.

Try this: Get rid of every device, pod and refill tonight.

When a craving hits

A craving rises, peaks and fades, usually within a few minutes. AirAgain’s SOS screen is made for those minutes: guided breathing, a quick game to take your mind off it and your own reasons for quitting, one tap away. Log what set it off, and you start to see your own triggers.

How a craving works

A craving is like a wave: it builds, peaks and falls away, usually within a few minutes. Giving in doesn't make cravings stop; it teaches your brain that the wave always ends with nicotine.

A simple plan for the next wave is the "4 Ds": Delay for a few minutes, breathe Deeply, Drink a glass of water, Do something else with your hands.

Try this: Use the 4 Ds on your next craving and log it in SOS.

Try these

  • Drink a glass of cold water.
  • Take a short 5-minute walk.
  • Chew gum or eat some fruit.
  • Keep your hands busy: write or doodle.
  • Text a friend.
  • Remember why you quit.

Count what you keep

Vaping costs add up one pod or disposable at a time. In vape mode, AirAgain asks what a pod or disposable costs you and how many days one lasts. From the first minute, it counts the money you keep, the pods you don’t buy and the puffs you don’t take.

Pick something to save for. Smokefree.gov suggests thinking about how you’ll spend your savings to get through a craving, and AirAgain shows how close your goal is.

When to get help

You don’t have to quit alone. Talk to a doctor, pharmacist or your local stop-smoking service, especially if you’ve tried before or withdrawal is hard. Smokefree.gov notes that quit-smoking medicines are not approved for quitting vaping in the US, but a health professional can tell you whether one might be right for you.

Free quitlines help people who vape too: the CDC points vapers to trained quit coaches, and the WHO recommends national toll-free quitlines. AirAgain’s health screen shows the free quitline or stop-smoking service for your country, where there is one.

If your mood drops a lot or you feel very low, don’t keep it to yourself. Tell someone close to you and talk to a doctor.
